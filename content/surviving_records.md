## Why some players are only a surname

This database holds 413 players with no recorded first name. Eight of them are known only as Smith, and dozens more are known by a bare initial. They appear throughout the app with a **Partial record** badge.

They are not data-entry errors. Each of them played professional baseball and appeared in a box score that survived, but left nothing else that researchers have found so far. Most debuted between 1910 and 1949, in the segregated Black leagues. A smaller group dates to the 1870s–1900s, when even the white professional game kept uneven books.

In other words, whether a player's record survived was never neutral. It depended on who wrote the games down, which newspapers printed them, and whose archives were kept.

## A record kept by weekly newspapers

The National and American Leagues had daily press coverage in every city. For most of their history they also had central statistical bureaus tallying every game. Black baseball had neither.

Big-city dailies rarely printed Negro Leagues box scores. The record fell mostly to the Black press, papers like the *Chicago Defender* and the *Pittsburgh Courier*, which published weekly, not daily. A box score might run days after the game, without first names, or not at all.

League schedules were also only part of the season. Teams sustained themselves by barnstorming: exhibition games, town-team challenges, and winter play in Cuba and Mexico. None of that entered a league ledger. The official schedule ran roughly 60–80 league games; the men on the field often played twice that many.

## What the fragments mean: Josh Gibson

Josh Gibson's Hall of Fame plaque credits him with "almost 800 home runs" across all competition: league games, exhibitions and barnstorming tours together. The documented league record in this database credits him with **197 home runs and a .364 average** across 1930–1946.

Both numbers are honest; they measure different things. The plaque counts a career as it was lived. The database counts the part of it that surviving box scores can prove.

That gap is the surviving-record problem in one player. The greatest documented totals of the Negro Leagues consistently understate the careers behind them, because the seasons were short and much of the play was never recorded by a league. Comparing a Negro Leagues counting stat directly against a 154-game AL/NL season misreads what the number represents.

## The reconstruction, still underway

What survives today exists because researchers rebuilt it game by game. The [Seamheads Negro Leagues Database](https://www.seamheads.com/NegroLgs/), led by Gary Ashwill and colleagues, rebuilds season lines from digitized newspaper box scores. Its totals still shift as more papers are scanned and more games are found.

In December 2020, Major League Baseball recognized seven Negro Leagues (1920–1948) as major leagues. In 2024 it added the reconstructed statistics to its official records, where Gibson's career batting average now stands as the all-time mark.

You can see the reconstruction's moving edge in this data. Different editions of the data disagree in small ways, such as an average a few points apart or a season found or refined. That isn't because anyone is wrong; the count is still being taken, a century later.

## How this app presents it

We show the documented record as it stands: raw totals, not adjusted for context. We mark its gaps instead of smoothing them over. Players for whom only a partial name survived carry the badge that brought you here.

Treat every segregation-era total as a floor, not a measure: the record that survived is the least these players did.
